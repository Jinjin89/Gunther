## The trial defends 2.1 °C of top-floor comfort and a 9% adjusted saving, not 18%
- **Weather-adjusted saving**: 9% once each summer is normalised by cooling degree days, against 18% as reported; roughly half the headline was weather [1]
- **Top-floor comfort**: Top-floor flats 2.1 °C cooler than matched untreated buildings; ground floors show no measurable change [3][4]
- **Street-level effect**: 0.1 more city-wide albedo lowers summer afternoon air by only about 0.3 °C — an indoor comfort measure [5]
- **Upkeep and glare**: Albedo fell to 0.55 after 18 months as dust and soot settled; 6 of 11 managers had received glare complaints [6][7]
Note: Four findings carry this briefing. Weather adjustment cuts the cooling saving from 18% to 9% [1], and the Climate Plan cites the 18% as the basis of its target [8] — that is the number the committee has to fix. The comfort result stands: top-floor flats ran 2.1 °C cooler than matched untreated buildings in the same hours [3], while ground-floor flats showed no measurable change [4]. On the street the effect is about 0.3 °C per 0.1 of albedo [5], which is why this is a comfort measure and not heat-island relief. Two maintenance facts decide whether the 9% holds: albedo fell to 0.55 in 18 months as dust and soot settled and the roofs were not cleaned during the trial [6], and six of eleven managers had received glare complaints [7]. I will take the energy number apart first.

## Weather adjustment halves the saving to 9%, and the plan's target still assumes 18%
| Cooling-energy saving | Saving (%) |
| --- | --- |
| Raw, as first reported | 18 [3] |
| **Weather-adjusted** | 9 [1] |
About half the original headline came from summer weather, including the unadjusted 2022 drought summer [1][6]
Note: The 18% figure is real, it is simply not a like-for-like comparison. Cooling demand rises with heat, and the 2022 drought summer fell in the middle of the trial and was never formally separated from the coating's effect [6][1]. Okafor normalised each building's cooling electricity by that summer's cooling degree days and re-ran the same matched comparison, getting 9% — about half the original headline came from the weather rather than the coating [1][9]. The Climate Plan cites 18% as the expected reduction in cooling electricity for coated buildings [8], and the 2028 review is the moment at which the council can adjust target, subsidy or both if observed savings sit far from the assumed figure [10]. So every kWh, carbon and payback figure derived from 18% has to be reworked. What does not move is the temperature result, which is the next slide.

## The 2.1 °C top-floor result survives reanalysis unchanged
**2.1 °C drop**
Peak indoor temperature fall in top-floor flats vs matched untreated buildings [3][1]
Sensors logged every 15 minutes and are compared within the same hours, so the result barely depends on which summer it was [1][11]
Note: This is the number the committee can defend without qualification. Temperature is measured directly and compared between treated and matched untreated buildings within the same hours, so it is far less sensitive to the weather of a given summer [1][11], and Okafor's reanalysis returns the same 2.1 °C as Harlow's original paper [1][3]. That matters politically: the committee can concede the energy figure without conceding the programme, because what residents actually notice — a top-floor flat that is bearable in August — is measured, not modelled. The open question is where inside the block that benefit lands, which is what I turn to now.

## The gain is concentrated in top-floor flats, with none on ground floors
| Floor instrumented | Temperature change (°C) |
| --- | --- |
| Top floor | 2.1 [3] |
| **Ground floor** | No measurable change [4] |
Ground-floor flats showed no measurable change; sensors sat in sample top- and ground-floor flats of each building [11][4]
Note: The benefit is concentrated by floor. Top-floor flats fell 2.1 °C against matched untreated buildings, while ground-floor flats in the same blocks showed no measurable change, because heat from the roof is largely lost before it reaches the lowest storeys and those flats are cooled by the ground and by shade [3][4]. Sensors covered the top and ground floors, so this deck claims nothing about middle floors [11]. The practical consequence is targeting. Top-floor heat is the single most common summer complaint reaching the city's housing advice service [8], and the modelled effect is largest in dense districts with little vegetation [5]. Coating every eligible roof spreads money over buildings where most residents gain nothing measurable. Next, what this does and does not do for the street.

## Street air cools about 0.3 °C, so stop selling this as heat-island relief
### Indoors, under a coated roof
- Peak indoor temperature in top-floor flats fell 2.1 °C vs matched untreated buildings [3]
- Sato: a flat directly under a treated roof can be several degrees cooler [5]
### Street air, city-wide
- A 0.1 rise in albedo lowers average summer afternoon air by about 0.3 °C [5]
- Model assumes steady reflectance and wind, ignoring reflected sunlight on pedestrians [12]
0.3 °C of air is hard to notice, and the model does not count reflected sunlight on pedestrians that makes a street feel hotter [5][12]
Note: Here is the claim I would retire. Sato's neighbourhood model puts a 0.1 rise in city-wide albedo at about 0.3 °C of average summer afternoon air temperature, close to linear [5] — against a 2.1 °C fall in peak indoor temperature in Velmora's top-floor flats [3]. Sato's own reading is that a cool roof is mainly a benefit to the people who live under it and only slightly to the people who walk past it [5]. And 0.3 °C is generous: the model assumes roofs keep their reflectance, though in practice it declines, treats wind as steady, and leaves out the effect of reflected sunlight on pedestrians, which can make a street feel hotter even when the air is cooler [12]. Presented as heat-island relief, the subsidy will be measured against a street temperature it cannot move. That raises the question of what the target actually buys.

## At 9%, the 30% target delivers half the promised saving: rebase it or double the area
### Rebase the assumed saving
- Keep the target of 30% of residential roofs by 2030; restate the assumed saving at 9% [1][13]
- The 2028 review revisits the target, subsidy or both if observed savings fall short [10]
### Hold the saving, double area
- The same kWh reduction needs roughly 60% of eligible roofs coated [1][13]
- At €14 per m², subsidy outlay roughly doubles [2]
The plan counts roofs, not floor area, and the subsidy is €14 per m² — fix the counting basis at the review [13][2][10]
Note: If a coated building saves 9% rather than 18%, the target delivers half the kWh the plan assumed from the same roof area [1][8]. The fall from 0.71 to 0.55 describes a progressively dirty roof; managers say coatings need washing every two years [15]
Note: The fall from 0.71 to 0.55 is not a steady-state number. Albedo went from 0.12 on the original dark roofs to 0.71 at treatment [11], then fell to 0.55 after 18 months because the trial never cleaned the roofs [6]. Managers say the coating needs washing every two years to keep its reflectance, two people for a morning, and that the cost is small next to the coating itself [15] — but it is a new task that does not fit existing maintenance contracts, and some managers admitted the cleaning was forgotten in the first year [15]. The subsidy is paid once, after inspection, so today nobody is answerable for keeping the reflectance up [2]. Making cleaning at 18 months, or a reflectance floor, with a named owner, a condition of the €14 per m² buys the thing that preserves the return. The other unpriced cost is glare.

Note: Glare was the most frequent complaint in the 2024 manager interviews. Six of eleven managers had at least one complaint about sunlight reflected into neighbours' windows in late afternoon, two were pressed by the neighbours' association, and none found a fully satisfactory solution [7]. The cheap fix is a planning rule: consider the height of surrounding buildings before choosing which roofs to coat, and allow a less reflective finish where roofs are overlooked [7]. ## Keep the programme and the 30% target, rebase the saving to 9%, and gate the subsidy
1. **Restate the saving**: Restate the saving at 9% and rework the derived carbon and payback figures before 2028 [1][8][10]
2. **Gate the €14 per m²**: Pay only where cleaning every 2 years, or a minimum reflectance level, has a named owner [6][15][2]
3. **Adopt a glare rule**: Limit full-reflectance finishes on overlooked roofs; add glare to the monitoring sample [7][10]
4. **Reframe it publicly**: Sell it as top-floor indoor comfort, not street-level heat-island relief [5][3]
Note: Four decisions. First, restate the plan's assumed saving as 9% and rework the derived carbon and payback figures before the 2028 review, which is the point at which the council decides whether to adjust target, subsidy or both [1][8][10]. Second, keep the 30% target rather than drop the measure [13]. Third, pay the €14 per m² only where cleaning every 2 years, or a minimum reflectance level, has a named owner, and adopt a glare rule for overlooked roofs with glare added to the monitoring sample [6][15][2][7][10]. Fourth, say publicly what this buys: top-floor indoor comfort, not street-level heat-island relief [5][3]. The 2.1 °C is ours to keep; the 18% is not [1].
