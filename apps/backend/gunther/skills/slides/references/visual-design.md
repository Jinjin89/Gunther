# Visual design method

Use this method to make the structure already present in the material easier to read. It is a
way to choose among the supported layouts and text forms, not permission to add visual claims.

## Decide what the content is doing

Before choosing a visual treatment, identify:

- **Hierarchy:** the page's main message, the evidence or explanation that supports it, and any
  qualifications that must stay visible.
- **Grouping:** which items are peers, and which parts belong together. Keep peers at equal visual
  weight unless the source itself establishes a meaningful difference.
- **Reading path:** whether the reader compares aligned attributes, follows an ordered sequence,
  or traces explicit directed relationships.
- **Focal point:** the existing phrase, number, row, or node that best carries the page's message.
  A focal point helps orient attention; it does not establish that the item is better, more common,
  more important, or more successful.

If the material does not establish an ordering, relationship, or distinction, keep that meaning
neutral and use spacing, alignment, restrained surfaces, dividers, scale, or typography to make the
page easier to scan. These visual devices may organize or pace information without representing a
new fact. Use them when they have a clear grouping, navigation, location, or emphasis role; avoid
ornament that competes with the page's message. Do not invent business entities, factual icons,
data relationships, categories, outcomes, or connectors.

## Use the supported forms

- A two-column table keeps each row paired across both columns. Use equal column treatment for
  peers; use row alignment to make comparison easy. Do not style one side as a winner without
  evidence and wording that support that conclusion.
- Cards collect a short list of peers. Give each card the same name/body hierarchy and similar
  space. Their order is the source or outline order, not a ranking.
- An ordered flow uses the existing numbered list. The renderer places 2–4 steps horizontally and
  5–6 vertically; its direction follows the item count, not a free layout choice. When an item
  begins with `**Step name**:` or another bold start, let that phrase identify the step and let
  the remaining words explain it. Keep every step, condition, qualification, and citation.
- A diagram uses only the directed edges already stated in its relation table. Let the graph's
  topology show starts, branches, and destinations. Do not add grouping headings, inferred edges,
  causal meaning, or a preferred route.
- `lead` and `after` prose remains supporting context. Keep its wording and citations; do not
  recast it as a conclusion, takeaway, caption, or claim unless the words themselves say that.

Choose a visual rhythm that fits the page: an open layout for a few important elements, a denser
aligned layout for a compact comparison, equal grouped areas for peers, or a clear focal area when
one existing claim or figure leads. Explain in the design guidance why that rhythm suits the
content. Realize it with the supported layout and composition, semantic roles, spacing, alignment,
type size and weight, and existing bold starts. The renderer does not support arbitrary node
positioning or new layout families.

Use the selected theme's shared semantic roles for grouping surfaces, supporting text, lines,
connections, and local focus. The accent may be neutral. Never rely on color alone: retain text,
position, alignment, numbering, line style, or weight that makes the relationship readable. A shape
or color wash may provide cadence or grouping, but must not imply ranking, causality, risk, or
success unless the source supports that meaning.

## Preserve meaning and evidence

Design fields are presentation guidance, not evidence. Use them to arrange or emphasize claims
that the page already has. Cite facts in the visible text in the normal way; design guidance does
not carry citation numbers. Preserve the user's words, Markdown formatting, and every citation.
Do not omit content to create whitespace. When a page is too dense, improve grouping and the
reading path first; then use the existing fit and layout fallback behavior without shrinking away
a qualification or reference.
