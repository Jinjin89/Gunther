# Velmora Cool Roof Trial (Harlow 2024)

## Background

Velmora is a mid-sized city whose summers have grown steadily hotter over the past two decades. Residents of the older apartment blocks in the river districts report that their top-floor flats become hard to live in from late June, and the municipal utility has recorded a rising share of its summer load that comes from air conditioning. Against this background the city's environment office asked a small research group, led by Dr. Anneke Harlow, to test whether a reflective roof coating could reduce indoor heat and the electricity spent on cooling.

The idea behind a cool roof is simple. A dark roof absorbs most of the sunlight that reaches it and passes much of that heat into the building below. A roof with a bright, reflective surface sends a large part of the sunlight back to the sky. The share of sunlight a surface reflects is called its albedo. Cool roofs have been tried in many warm cities, but the evidence from mid-sized European cities with a changeable climate was thin, and the city did not want to commit public money on the basis of studies from other climates alone. The trial reported here was designed to give Velmora its own numbers.

## Method

The trial covered 412 residential buildings across six districts, chosen with the help of the housing associations that own most of them. The buildings were followed over the summers 2021 to 2023, from the first of June to the thirty-first of August in each year. Buildings were selected so that they differed in height, in age and in the colour of their original roofs, which allowed the group to look at how the effect varies from one kind of building to another.

Each roof was treated with a white elastomeric coating, a paint-like layer that is rolled on in two passes and stays flexible as the roof expands and contracts with the weather. Roof albedo was raised from 0.12 to 0.71 on the treated roofs. Surface reflectance was measured on a sample of roofs with a handheld reflectometer before treatment and again at the start of each summer.

Indoor air temperature was logged every fifteen minutes by small sensors placed in a sample of flats on the top floor and on the ground floor of each building. Electricity use for cooling was taken from the utility's smart-meter records, using the flats that had an air-conditioning unit connected to a separately metered circuit. Comparison buildings with untreated roofs, matched on height and age, were monitored in the same way, so that differences between summers could be separated from the effect of the coating.

## Results

Peak indoor temperature in top-floor flats fell by 2.1 °C compared with the matched untreated buildings. The effect appeared in the first summer and was present on most hot days, being largest during the hottest afternoons, when the roof of an untreated building would otherwise have been radiating heat into the flat below for many hours. Residents who took part in short surveys reported that they used fans and portable coolers less often, and that bedrooms were easier to sleep in during heat waves.

Energy use followed the same pattern. Electricity for cooling fell by 18% across the trial buildings. Because the figure is an average over buildings that differ in size and in how much air conditioning they had installed to begin with, the savings were spread unevenly: a minority of buildings with large, well-used cooling systems accounted for a disproportionate share of the reduction, while buildings with no air conditioning at all, of which there were many, show up only in the temperature records and not in the electricity data. The utility noted that the saving was also visible in the evening peak of its summer load, although the group did not attempt to put a monetary value on that benefit.

The same sensors that recorded the top-floor result also covered the lower floors, and the picture there was different. Ground-floor flats showed no measurable change. Heat from the roof is largely lost before it reaches the lowest storeys of a tall block, and ground-floor flats are in any case cooled by the ground and by shade from neighbouring buildings, so the roof contributes little to their summer temperature in the first place.

## Limitations

Three limitations should be kept in mind when the numbers are used. The summer of 2022 was a drought summer, hotter and sunnier than the other two, and the trial design did not separate this from the effect of the coating in a formal way. Roof albedo did not stay at its initial value: it fell to 0.55 after 18 months because of dust and soot that settled on the surface, and the group did not clean the roofs during the trial. The figures in this report therefore describe a coating that was gradually becoming less reflective. Finally, only residential buildings were included, with no offices, so the results should not be read as applying to commercial buildings, which have different occupancy patterns and different cooling systems.

The group recommends that any follow-up should include a cleaning schedule, should add buildings of other types, and should adjust the energy figures for weather before they are compared with studies from other places or other years.
