# The winter heating penalty of cool roofs (Brandt 2022)

## The question

A reflective roof keeps heat out in summer. In winter it does the same thing at the wrong time: sunlight that a dark roof would have absorbed and passed into the building is sent back, and the heating system has to make up the difference. This note reviews what is known about that winter penalty and asks how large it is in different climates. It is intended for planners who have to weigh the summer benefit of cool roofs against a cost that is paid in the cold months.

## What the studies show

The studies reviewed here are building simulations and a small number of measurements on real buildings in North America, northern Europe and East Asia. They agree on the direction of the effect and differ on its size. In cold climates cool roofs raise heating demand by 3–7%. The figure is larger where winters are long and heating is the main energy use of a building, and smaller where winters are short or cloudy and there is little sun to lose.

Three things make the penalty smaller than a simple calculation suggests. In winter the sun is low and the days are short, so there is little sunlight to be reflected. Roofs are often covered by snow or leaves, which hides the coating. And in a well-insulated building the roof's surface temperature has only a small effect on how much heat the rooms below need. The penalty is largest for poorly insulated buildings with a single storey and a roof that is large compared with the walls.

## Mild climates

For places with mild winters the penalty is much smaller, and the summer benefit weighs more. The note includes an estimate for the climate of Velmora, a mid-sized European city with mild winters, using typical weather data and a standard model of an apartment block of five storeys. For Velmora's mild winters the estimated heating penalty is +1.2%. This is a modelled figure, not a measured one, and it is calculated for the whole of the building's heating demand, not just the top floor.

## How to use the numbers

The author suggests that planners should compare the penalty and the benefit in the same units, such as kilowatt-hours per square metre of roof per year, and should take into account that electricity for cooling and fuel for heating may have different prices and different carbon content. In most mild climates the summer saving is a few times larger than the winter penalty, so that the net effect of a cool roof is positive; in cold climates it may be close to zero, and a different measure should be considered.
