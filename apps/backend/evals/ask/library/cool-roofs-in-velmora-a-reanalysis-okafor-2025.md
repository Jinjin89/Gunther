# Cool roofs in Velmora: a reanalysis (Okafor 2025)

## Purpose

This note reports a reanalysis of the data collected in the Velmora cool roof trial published by Harlow in 2024. The original trial attracted wide attention because its headline figures were large and because the city's planners began to use them almost immediately. The aim of the reanalysis was narrow: to ask whether the headline numbers still hold when the data are adjusted for the weather of each summer, using a standard method from building energy research.

## Method

Cooling demand depends heavily on how hot the weather is. A building that uses a given amount of electricity in a mild summer will use much more in a hot one, whether or not its roof has been treated. Comparing one summer with another without allowing for this can make a measure look better or worse than it is. The standard remedy is to express demand in relation to cooling degree days, a count that adds up, day by day, how far the outdoor temperature rose above the level at which buildings start to need cooling. Dividing electricity use by cooling degree days gives a figure that can be compared across summers of different severity.

The reanalysis used the same electricity records and the same matched untreated buildings as the original trial. No new measurements were taken. Each building's cooling electricity was normalised by the cooling degree days of the summer in question, and the saving was then recalculated as the difference between treated and untreated buildings in these normalised terms.

## Findings

With weather adjustment, the cooling-energy saving is 9%, not 18%. About half of the saving reported in the original paper therefore comes from differences in the weather between the summers and not from the coating itself.

The reanalysis points out that Harlow did not adjust for the 2022 drought summer. That summer was unusually hot and sunny, and it fell in the middle of the trial period. A comparison that does not allow for the heat of that summer therefore mixes the effect of the coating with the effect of the weather.

The reanalysis agrees with the 2.1 °C indoor temperature result. Temperature is measured directly and compared between treated and untreated buildings within the same hours, so it is much less sensitive to differences in weather between summers, and the adjusted analysis returns the same value as the original.

## Discussion

The author stresses that the smaller saving does not mean that the coating is a poor investment. A saving of 9% across a large number of buildings is still substantial, and the effect on indoor comfort is unchanged. What changes is the figure that should be used in planning documents and in comparisons with other cities. Anyone quoting the energy saving from the trial should say whether it is the raw or the weather-adjusted number.

The note also lists what the reanalysis cannot do. It cannot correct for the gradual loss of reflectance of the roofs, because reflectance was measured only on a sample, and it cannot say whether the saving would be larger or smaller in a mild summer, since the three summers in the data were all warmer than the long-term average. A longer record, collected with a cleaning schedule, would settle both questions.
