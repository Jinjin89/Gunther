# Albedo and urban air temperature: a modelling note (Sato 2021)

## Purpose

This note uses a neighbourhood-scale climate model to ask a question that is often skipped when reflective surfaces are promoted: how much does raising the albedo of a city's roofs cool the air that people breathe and walk through? The question is different from the one asked in building studies, which look at the temperature indoors, and it matters for the argument that cool roofs reduce the urban heat island, the tendency of built-up areas to be warmer than their surroundings.

## Model

The model represents a mid-sized city as a grid of blocks, each with a given share of roof, road, pavement and vegetation, and each exchanging heat with the air above. It was run for a typical hot summer using weather observations from a European city on the same latitude as the cities in the study. Because the model is simple it can be run many times with different assumptions, which makes it useful for comparing options, but it cannot reproduce the fine detail of a single street.

Albedo was changed in steps, applied to all roofs in the model at once, and the response of the air temperature at head height in the afternoon, when the heat island is strongest, was recorded for each step.

## Results

Raising city-wide albedo by 0.1 lowers average summer afternoon air temperature by about 0.3 °C. The relation is close to linear over the range tested, so that a rise of 0.2 gives roughly twice the cooling. The effect is largest in dense districts with little vegetation, and smallest in districts that are already green and open.

The effect at street level is small compared with indoor effects in top-floor flats. A change of 0.3 °C in the air outside is hard to notice, while a flat directly under a roof can be several degrees cooler when that roof reflects sunlight. In other words, a cool roof is mainly a benefit to the people who live under it, and only slightly to the people who walk past it.

## Caveats

Three caveats apply. The model assumes that roofs keep their reflectance, whereas in practice it declines as roofs become dirty. It treats the wind as steady, which tends to understate the mixing of warm and cool air in real streets. And it does not include the effect of reflected sunlight on pedestrians, which can make the street feel hotter even if the air is cooler. The author recommends that cities that want to lower air temperatures should combine reflective roofs with trees and shade, since these act where people are.
